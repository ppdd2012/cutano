Print-ready sticker cut-outs

A sticker needs real transparency. Here's what that file is — and what it isn't.

Turning a photo into something a print shop or a cutting service can use comes down to two things a screen preview hides from you: alpha where the background used to be, and enough pixels to survive the printed size. This page covers both, and states plainly where a PNG cut-out stops being the whole job.

The actual requirement

What a print shop or cutting service needs from your file

Two things, and only two: the area outside the subject must be genuinely transparent, and the image must carry enough pixels for the size it will be printed at. Everything else on this page is detail that supports those two.

Alpha, not white

A transparent PNG stores a per-pixel alpha channel, so the file itself declares "nothing goes here". The printer doesn't have to guess, and whatever is underneath — coloured vinyl, a kraft backing, a pattern — shows through exactly where you expect.

Why a white box is not the same thing

A subject sitting on a white background looks identical on screen and behaves completely differently downstream. White is ink, or the absence of it, and a shop has to knock it out by selecting the white — which eats highlights on the subject itself, and leaves a visible white plate if the removal is imperfect.

Enough pixels for the printed size

Resolution only means anything once you know how big the thing will be. On Pro, Cutano exports at your photo's original resolution up to 8192px; the free tier exports at a reduced resolution. PNG and JPG are yours to choose on every tier, layered PSD on Pro.

The honest section

Raster vs vector: what Cutano gives you is pixels

This is where a lot of sticker guides get vague, so here it is stated directly. A PNG cut-out is a rectangle of coloured pixels plus an alpha channel. An SVG is a set of shapes described mathematically. They are different kinds of object, and a blade-plotter reads the second one.

What a PNG cut-out is genuinely the right file for

  • Any printed sticker, decal or label — the shop prints the pixels and cuts to the shape it can see
  • Sheet layouts where many cut-outs sit on one printed page
  • Scaling a large export down to a small sticker, which keeps detail
  • Anything a person opens in Photoshop, Affinity, Canva or a print portal

Where you will need a separate step

  • A blade plotter needs a vector outline; a PNG does not contain one
  • To get that outline, trace the alpha shape into paths in a vector or cutting tool
  • Tracing is a conversion step done by you in that ecosystem's software — it is not built into Cutano
  • The practical handoff: export a clean high-resolution transparent PNG, then trace it. The better the edge, the better the trace.

Put simply: Cutano does not export SVG, DXF or any cut-file format, and it has no integration with a cutting machine or a print portal. It gives you the image half of a sticker job done properly, and the shape half is yours to make in a tool built for it.

Size it before you export

Getting the printed size right — it's one line of arithmetic

DPI is not a property stored inside a file like a name is. It is a relationship: how many of your pixels should be squeezed into one inch of paper. The formula is just pixels = inches × DPI, and you can invert it whenever you already have the pixels.

A small sticker is easy

A 3-inch sticker at 300 DPI needs 3 × 300 = 900 pixels on that side. A phone photo is many times that, so a typical die-cut sticker has more than enough to spare.

8192px is a lot of print

At 300 DPI, 8192 ÷ 300 ≈ 27 inches, or about 69 cm on the long side — the Pro original-resolution ceiling. That is well past sticker scale, covering a large print sheet with headroom still left over.

Don't trust the DPI number

Many files carry a DPI value that is only a printed-size suggestion in metadata. It changes no pixels. What decides sharpness is total pixels divided by the size you actually asked for.

The warning that goes with this: upscaling a small file does not create detail that was never captured. Interpolation smears and softens; it cannot invent an edge. That is exactly why exporting at original resolution matters for print rather than screen — a print file can always be scaled down cleanly, but it cannot be scaled up into sharpness. Export the biggest clean version you have and let the print engine shrink it.

Where stickers actually fail

Edge quality is what ruins a printed sticker

On screen, a thin ring of leftover background is invisible against a dark interface. Once printed and cut, that same ring becomes a coloured outline tracing your subject — the single most common reason a homemade sticker looks homemade. The fix is knowing which control solves which symptom.

What you seeWhy it happensWhat to use
Coloured halo following the outlineBackground pixels left inside the maskEdge contraction — eat it inward
Soft grey rim after print and cutFeathering pushed too far; semi-transparent pixels print as a washLess feathering, then re-check at print size

A piece of the subject missingSegmentation dropped itRestore brush, non-destructive with undo
Something extra still attachedSegmentation kept itErase brush, then contract by a hair

The rule of thumb worth repeating: contraction fixes the halo, feathering only smooths it, and heavy feathering is the thing that turns into a grey rim under ink. Contract first, feather lightly, and always judge the result at the size it will print — not at the size it sits in a preview. Refinement happens with the erase and restore brushes, which are non-destructive, so a wrong call is one undo away rather than a restart.

Sticker convention

The white border, and the choke

Sticker work has a convention that catches people out on their first sheet: a thin opaque border around the shape, slightly wider than the cut line, so the blade can wander a fraction without exposing a sliver of backing where colour should be.

How the convention works

  • A border of white (or the backing colour) is offset outward around the silhouette
  • Cut tolerance means the blade may drift a fraction of a millimetre; the border hides that drift
  • The "choke" is the opposite move: pulling the printed shape very slightly inside the cut so a misregistration cannot show a gap
  • Kiss-cut sheets, contour-cut labels and half-cut decals each have their own version of the same idea

What this has to do with the app

  • The border is not drawn for you — the app has no automatic offset or border feature
  • Choke and offset values are set by your printer or in the cutting software, not here
  • What the app does give you is the clean transparent shape you build that border around
  • A tight, tidy edge is what makes an offset border look deliberate instead of accidental

If a shop asks you for a bleed or a border specification, that's a conversation about their press and their die, not about your cut-out. Send the transparent PNG they asked for, ask what border they apply, and let them handle it — most sticker printers would rather add their own border than inherit yours.

Counter-intuitive but important

For a cut file, turn the shadows off

AI contact shadow, shadow blur and a gradient-faded floor reflection are the features that make a product composite look grounded and lit on screen. On a sticker they are pure liability: the shadow is semi-transparent grey pixels, and semi-transparent grey pixels print as a haze your shop cannot easily remove.

Exporting a file to be printed and cut

  • Leave contact shadow off — anything outside the silhouette is not a sticker, it is a smudge
  • Leave floor reflection off for the same reason
  • Check the alpha on a coloured background before you send the file; white-on-white hides leftovers
  • You want one shape, one opaque subject, zero grey around it

Exporting a mockup or a listing image

  • Keep the shadow — the point of a mockup is to look like a real object on a real surface
  • A reflection is desirable for a glossy product shot and reads as studio work
  • Anything printed on a solid background is unforgiving of a hard cut-out edge
  • But never send that shadowed version to be die-cut

Same photo, two different exports: one is a cut file, one is a presentation. The features are worth switching off and on rather than picking a side permanently.

FAQ

The questions this actually raises

Can I use this with a Cricut or a Silhouette?
You can use its output, with one extra step. Import your photo, cut it out, export a transparent PNG, then open that PNG in the cutting software and use its image-tracing feature to generate a cut line. There's no direct integration and nothing here is a supported pairing with any machine — the PNG is simply a clean, high-resolution shape for that software to trace.
What file should I actually send the print shop?
Transparent PNG is the safe default — it carries the alpha and survives the round trip. Send a layered PSD (Pro) if their designer asked to keep the layers editable. Don't send JPG for a die-cut, because JPG has no transparency at all; the "background" becomes whatever white you photographed or filled in.
How many DPI do I really need?
Work it backwards from the size. Multiply the printed inches you want by the DPI and that's your pixel requirement: 3 in at 300 DPI is 900 px. 300 DPI is the usual expectation for close-up print, and around 150 DPI is often accepted for large-format work viewed from a distance. If you export at original resolution on Pro (up to 8192px), you have 27 inches of 300-DPI print to spend.
Why is there a coloured line around my sticker?
Almost always leftover background inside the mask that you couldn't see against the app's preview. Nudge edge contraction to pull the mask inward past the fringe. If instead the outline looks soft and grey, that's over-feathering — semi-transparent pixels print as a wash — so bring feathering back down and re-check on a coloured background.
Can the app make an SVG?
No, and it's worth saying clearly. Cutano exports raster files — transparent PNG, high-resolution JPG and, on Pro, layered PSD. It doesn't produce SVG or vector paths, and it won't hand you a cutting file. If a vector is what your workflow needs, trace the alpha edge of your PNG in a vector or cutting tool as a separate step.
