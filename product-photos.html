The workflow problem

Every listing asks for two different pictures

Most marketplaces want an isolated main image on a plain background, plus a separate set of supporting shots that show the thing in use. That's not a style preference — it's two jobs, and a single photograph can't do both at once.

The main image

The product alone, filling a square frame on a plain backdrop. It's the tile shoppers see in search results, sitting directly next to your competitors, so it gets compared at thumbnail size before anyone reads a word.

The supporting shots

Angle views, in-hand scale, the product on a table or on a person. These carry context and mood — a background is a feature here, not a defect, and a prop or a caption helps.

Why one photo can't do both

The frame that reads well as a lifestyle shot is the frame that fails as a thumbnail: busy backdrop, uneven light, product at a third of the image. You need the same subject twice, in two different costumes.

This is why cutout work is the hinge of the whole process rather than a finishing touch. Isolate the subject once and you can put it back on plain white for the main image and onto a room scene for the second slot, with the same pixels both times. In Cutano that's the Product Showcase flow for the flat listing image, and multi-subject placement on a canvas when you want a context shot built from the same cut-out.

Pure white backgrounds

White is a number, not a colour you photographed

The convention for an isolated main image is pure white: R255 G255 B255 in every pixel behind the product. That's a specific value, and the reason it matters is that everything downstream — thumbnails, colour-managed browsers, comparison feeds — assumes the backdrop carries no information. Anything other than 255 on all three channels is telling the shopper something the listing didn't intend.

How to land on exact white

  • Cut the product out first, so the surface you photographed stops mattering at all
  • Fill the canvas with the app's background colour instead of keeping the shot's backdrop — this is what makes 255,255,255 reachable rather than approximate
  • Zoom in on the edge and check for a halo of the old background clinging to it
  • Export a flat image, not a transparent one, when the upload field wants a finished picture

Before you trust this page

  • Marketplaces revise their image specs, and the page you read last year may no longer be the rule
  • Minimum pixel counts, file-size caps and frame-fill percentages change without warning
  • Confirm the current requirement in your own seller dashboard on the day you upload, and treat this section as the general convention rather than the live spec
Canvas and aspect ratio

Work square from the start, and leave room at the edges

Selling layouts are grids. A square canvas is the shape that survives being tiled next to everyone else's square without anyone's product getting chopped. If you deliver a wide frame, someone else decides where the crop lands — usually through the middle of your packaging.

Pick the container before you place the product

  • Canvas presets cover the common cases, including an e-commerce hero frame, poster and short-video ratios
  • Original, 1:1, 9:16 and 16:9 are built in, plus custom sizes when a template asks for something else
  • One-tap smart crop fits the frame to the transparent subject, which is a fast starting point
  • Scaling the product up until it kisses the edges leaves nothing for the platform's own crop

Centred, with even margins

  • Drop the subject to roughly four fifths of the frame so a consistent band of white surrounds it on all four sides
  • Check the margins against the grid guides rather than by eye — uneven padding is the tell of an amateur listing
  • Pinch to scale, drag to place, double-tap to reset when you've nudged it crooked
  • Cropping tight to save "wasted" white usually costs you the thumbnail

Build the image at the ratio the grid expects and the empty space stops being wasted pixels — it becomes the breathing room that makes a small product legible at 120 pixels wide.

Grounding the product

The shadow is a decision, not a default

A cut subject with nothing under it can look weightless — pasted on. That's what contact shadows and floor reflections are for: they tell the eye the object is sitting on something. The useful, less-obvious skill is knowing when to leave both switched off.

Turn the shadow on

Secondary images, banners, ad creative and any context shot where the product shares a surface with a scene. A soft rendered contact shadow with a little blur is the difference between "placed" and "pasted", and it's what makes a phone capture look studio-lit.

Turn the reflection on

Glossy products on dark or graded floors, cosmetics, bottles, anything selling a premium feel. A gradient-faded reflection reads as a set. On a busy lifestyle background it usually disappears, so save it for clean compositions.

Turn both off

A main image on pure white. A cast shadow there is a blob of grey on your 255,255,255 field — the exact thing the plain-background convention exists to prevent. Long-standing listing and print practice is an isolated subject with no shadow at all.

Because the shadow and the reflection are separate renderings on separate elements, you can build both versions from one session: the clean unshadowed subject for the primary slot, the grounded one for the gallery. Some categories genuinely want a subtle drop shadow in the secondary images, so treat "no shadow on white" as the convention to default to and your dashboard's current guidance as the thing that overrides it.

Hard edges and shiny packaging

Why glossy products break naive segmentation

People are soft-edged and predictable. Products are the opposite: a specular highlight on a shrink-wrapped box is a patch of pure white sitting inside the object, and a matte black case on a dark table shares a colour with its own background. One-shot segmentation has to guess where the boundary is, and on packaging it often guesses wrong — clipping a corner off a bottle or leaving a grey rim of the old surface around the whole thing.

The failure you'll see

A bite taken out of a glossy shoulder, a hard edge left fuzzy, or a thin halo of the table clinging to the silhouette. On a white background every one of those becomes visible, which is why they matter more for listings than for anything else.

The fix that's automatic

Switch to the edge-refined matting model and re-run the same object in place, then keep whichever result is right. Different models are good at hair, at hard product edges, and at messy backgrounds, so a second opinion costs you a tap.

The fix that's manual

Nudge edge contraction to eat the remaining halo and feather to soften a cut that looks too brittle, then use the restore brush to bring back a corner the model discarded. It's non-destructive with undo, so a bad pass is one tap to reverse.

One shooting habit pays for all of this: put the product against a backdrop that contrasts with the product, not against white. A white box photographed on white paper is the hardest segmentation case that exists, and the cut-out means the shooting background never has to appear in the final image anyway.

Scaling a catalogue

Sixty SKUs is a batch job, not sixty errands

The reason sellers give up on doing their own images is the repetition, not the quality of any single frame. Doing it in bulk changes the shape of the work: one import, one pass, and then only the handful of failures need your attention.

Set the batch up to succeed

  • Shoot the whole range in one session with the same distance and the same light, so a single treatment fits all of them
  • Import the album and run matting across it in one go instead of opening each photo by hand
  • Keep shadows off for the pass that produces main images — you can ground selected shots later
  • Don't batch two different product colours against two different backgrounds; split those jobs

Then deal only with the rejects

  • Batch scheduling works through the queue by device capability, so a long album doesn't stall the phone
  • Retry the individual items that came back wrong rather than re-running everything
  • On a stubborn SKU, switch to the edge-refined model and re-matte that one object in place
  • Everything is computed on the device — no upload queue and no waiting on a connection while the range is in flight
Export for a listing

JPG, PNG or PSD — and why zoom needs real pixels

The zoom view on a product page magnifies whatever you actually exported. There's no rescue at that end: an image stretched from a small file stays soft, because enlarging can't invent detail that was never captured. So export at the photo's original resolution — up to 8192px on Pro — and let the platform do the downscaling.

For the upload field

  • JPG for the finished listing image: background already filled with white, no transparency to be reinterpreted, universally accepted, and small enough to upload
  • PNG when you want the subject on alpha — a second image slot, a size chart overlay, or a background you plan to change later
  • Flat image over layered file whenever a human isn't going to open it in a design program

For the handoff

  • Layered PSD (Pro) when someone else finishes the artwork — every layer stays editable in Photoshop instead of being flattened at your end
  • Keep the transparent PNG masters for a range so a future background change is a re-export, not a re-shoot
  • Export size and format are shown up front, never decided behind your back — the free tier's reduced resolution is labelled, not hidden
  • Listing images on the free plan carry a watermark, so run one test image before you commit a whole catalogue
Checklist

The run sheet for a batch of listing images

1. Shoot

Same distance, same light, one session. Backdrop that contrasts with the product. Fill the frame — resolution you export from is resolution you shot. Extras on a separate pass.

2. Isolate

Batch the album. For each result check the silhouette for halos and clipped corners, tighten edge contraction, restore anything discarded, re-matte the failures on the edge-refined model.

3. Compose

Square or e-commerce hero canvas. Subject centred at about four fifths of the frame, even margins on all sides, background filled with exact white, shadow and reflection switched off.

4. Build the gallery

Second set on real backgrounds, contact shadow and reflection on, text or props where they help. Save both sets to your library from the same cut-outs.

5. Export

Original resolution up to 8192px on Pro. JPG for uploads, transparent PNG for masters, layered PSD (Pro) if a designer takes it from here. Name files by SKU as you go.

6. Verify

Open the exported file and sample the backdrop — it should read 255,255,255 on all three channels. Then check today's image requirements in your seller dashboard before uploading.

FAQ

What sellers ask before they switch to doing it themselves

Can I honestly get listing-compliant photos from a phone camera?
For most small products, yes — the bottleneck was never the camera. A modern iPhone gives you more than enough detail for a listing image and the zoom view. What a phone can't give you by itself is a controlled backdrop and a square canvas, and that's the part the cutout workflow supplies. Where a phone genuinely struggles is very small reflective jewellery and large awkward items you can't get distance from.
How do I get the background truly white instead of grey?
Stop trying to photograph white and start filling it in. Cut the subject out, then set the canvas background to white yourself, so every pixel behind the product is the same value all the way to the corners. If a corner still looks grey, there's a gradient in the photo you kept — re-crop or re-matte and check for a halo around the edge.
What size should I actually make the image?
The durable answers are shape and margin: work on a square canvas, keep the product centred, leave an even band of white around it. The numeric part — minimum edge length, file-size ceiling — is exactly what platforms change, so read it from your own seller dashboard the day you upload rather than from a blog post of any age. Export at original resolution on Pro and you can always downscale to whatever number they're asking for this quarter.
I have hundreds of products. Can any of this be done in bulk?
Yes, and that's the only realistic way to do a catalogue yourself. Run matting across a whole album in one pass, then retry just the items that came back badly instead of starting over. Two things still need human eyes per SKU: whether the edge is clean, and whether the product is placed squarely with even margins. Everything before and after that is batch work.
Do I need a lightbox or a studio setup?
Not for the background — that's the whole point of isolating the subject, since the surface behind it never appears in the finished image. What still matters is even, soft light so the product has no harsh hotspots and no dark side, plus a fixed distance so a batch looks consistent. A window and a sheet of card is enough to start. A lightbox earns its keep on small glossy items, where controlling reflections is the actual difficulty.
